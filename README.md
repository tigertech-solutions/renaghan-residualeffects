# Residual Effects of Air Travel on Oxygen Saturation in Collegiate American Football Athletes

Eric J. Renaghan, Kyle M. Bellamy — University of Miami

Harrison L. Wittels, Gabriel Huang, Michael J. Wishon — Tiger Tech Solutions

## Abstract

Studies that track oxygen saturation (SpO2) during air travel measure it only in flight or shortly after landing, assuming it returns to baseline within minutes, and many use laboratory simulations that cannot reproduce the circadian disruption of crossing time zones. This study investigates whether SpO2 remains suppressed on the day after real team air travel, and whether the effect scales with the number of time zones crossed. Morning resting SpO2 was recorded in 47 NCAA Division I football players across 203 athlete-flights to 13 away games over three seasons. After long-haul flights crossing three time zones, mean SpO2 fell from 98.54% on the morning of the flight to 96.56% the next morning (−1.98 percentage points; p = 0.0014), whereas flights crossing zero or one time zone produced no change (between-group p = 0.0019). The decline held within athletes who flew both trip types (−2.22 points; p = 0.003), resolved by the second day after arrival, and grew by 0.72 points with each time zone crossed (p < 0.001). Long-haul air travel appears to have residual effects on SpO2 that outlast the flight. Teams traveling across three time zones may benefit from earlier departure, adjusted day-after workloads, and individual SpO2 monitoring.

## Data

This repository contains the de-identified data for this paper.

The file is `data/spo2-flights-anonymized.csv`. Each row is one athlete-flight. Athlete and trip numbers are anonymous, and the file does not include names, dates, opponents, or airports.
